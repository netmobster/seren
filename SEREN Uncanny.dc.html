Player chaos

No plan survives contact with the players.

Going off script isn't a failure mode of tabletop. It is the whole appeal. Players will always do the thing nobody prepared for — and a system that only knows the plan treats that as an error. It refuses, it railroads you back, or it quietly drops the thread and pretends the detour never happened. SEREN takes the detour as canon and keeps building from there.

What it is

A campaign in SEREN is not a transcript. It is a world — characters, factions, locations, objects, rules, secrets, consequences, unresolved threads, an evolving canon — and SEREN improvises freely inside it while keeping every part of it coherent, session after session.

It works in chat. There is no application to learn and no dashboard to maintain. And it is a Dungeon Master, not a playback device: you give it the bones, and it makes the calls in the moment — never asking you to remind it what already happened.

How it works

Three things, deliberately kept apart.

Most tools fuse the story, the rules and the current situation into one long conversation. SEREN separates them. That separation is the whole architecture — and the reason the system isn't bound to any single game.

World I
Your universe.

Canon, characters, places, factions, history, secrets, the things that are simply true. Yours — imported or grown at the table.

Ruleset II
The mechanics.

D&D 5e, with your house rules treated as canon. A ruleset is a layer, not the foundation — which is why it can be swapped.

State III
What is true now.

Every change your players caused, who witnessed it, what it cost, and what it set in motion. Live, and carried forward.

D&D is where SEREN starts. It isn't where SEREN ends.

Because the world, the rules and the live state are separate concerns, the architecture doesn't care which game you're playing. A science-fiction setting, an original universe, a system of your own — that is where this is going. Today it runs 5e, and it runs it properly.

Consequence

A death in session four is still a death in session forty.

One decision at the table doesn't resolve. It propagates — through people, places, factions and time — and the world goes on acting on it while nobody is looking.

Session 4 The party kills Noke.
Immediately Noke is dead. Permanently, and for everyone.
Same night Shinebright's future changes.
Day 3 The tower stands unclaimed.
Day 31 Local nobles begin to ask who owns it.
Session 9 A thread you never wrote is waiting for you.

SEREN doesn't forget the consequences just because the session ended. Nobody had to log any of this.

What it allows

Six things that were not possible before.

01
Continuity that holds

You never open a session by explaining your own world back to the machine. It already knows, and it knows what changed last time.

02
Characters who exist

Ser'en is a level 11 Circle of Stars druid. She is also someone with a debt, a grudge and a reason to distrust Kestrel. SEREN holds both, and neither contradicts the other.

03
Secrets kept correctly

Knowledge is tracked per person. An NPC cannot react to something they were never told, and the world cannot leak what the players haven't earned.

04
Consequences that compound

Factions move between sessions. Unclaimed power gets claimed. The world has business of its own and gets on with it.

05
Preparation that persists

Work you did once stays done. The village you built in session two is still there, still populated, still remembering the party's manners.

06
Your rules, honoured

House rulings become part of the ruleset rather than something you re-argue every week. Your table's law is the law.
